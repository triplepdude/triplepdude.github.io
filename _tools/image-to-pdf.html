Press **Choose images**, drop files onto the box, or paste a copied screenshot with Ctrl+V (⌘V on a Mac). You can keep adding more. 2. Put the pages in order. Drag a page by its grip handle (with a mouse you can drag the whole card) or use the arrow buttons. The × removes a page. 3. Pick a page size, orientation and margin. The thumbnails show exactly where each image will sit on its page. 4. Press **Create PDF**. The file is assembled on your device and saved to your downloads folder. Each image becomes one page. It is scaled to fit inside the margins, never cropped or stretched, and centred on the page. ## Page size, orientation and margin | Setting | Page size | |---|---| | A4 | 210 × 297 mm (595.28 × 841.89 pt) | | US Letter | 8.5 × 11 in (612 × 792 pt) | | Fit to image | The image at 96 pixels per inch, plus the margin | {: .data .itp-table} **Auto** orientation turns a page sideways when its image is wider than it is tall, so a landscape photo is not shrunk onto an upright sheet. Choose **Portrait** or **Landscape** to make every page the same, which matters when the PDF will be printed double-sided or bound. Most printers cannot print right to the edge of the paper and leave a few millimetres blank, so the default small margin (6 mm, about ¼ inch) keeps the edge of a scanned page from being cut off. Choose **None** for a document that will only be read on screen, or when your images already have white borders. Untick **Enlarge small images** to place small pictures, such as logos or icons, at their natural size instead of blowing them up to fill the page. ## What happens to image quality **JPEG photos are copied into the PDF byte for byte.** The PDF format can store JPEG data as it is (the `DCTDecode` filter in ISO 32000), so there is no second round of compression, no quality loss, and the PDF is only a few kilobytes larger than the photos themselves. Phone cameras often save the picture sideways plus an EXIF orientation tag that says how to turn it. The tool reads that tag and rotates the image with the page's transformation matrix instead of re-encoding it, so portrait photos come out upright. PNG, WebP, GIF and other formats are decoded by your browser, placed on a white background (transparent areas become white, as they would on paper) and stored as a JPEG at 92% quality. JPEG compression can leave faint smudges around sharp lettering, so for screenshots and scanned text save them as **Lossless**: the exact pixels are stored with lossless Flate compression. The file is bigger, but text stays crisp. Animated GIFs contribute their first frame, and CMYK JPEGs from print software are converted to RGB so their colours are right in every viewer. ## Tips - Photograph documents straight on in even light, and crop away the table or background first; this tool fits images but does not crop them. - The PDF is roughly as large as all your images together. To email a big batch, shrink the photos first: about 1,600 pixels on the long side is plenty for an A4 or Letter page read on screen. - The output is a standard PDF 1.4 file that opens in any PDF reader, including web browsers, Adobe Acrobat, macOS Preview and phone viewers. faq: - q: Are my images uploaded to a server? a: No. The images are read and the PDF is assembled by JavaScript running in this page, on your own device. Nothing is sent over the network, so the tool keeps working even if you go offline after the page has loaded. - q: How many images can I put in one PDF? a: There is no fixed limit. The images and the finished PDF are held in your device's memory, so very large batches, hundreds of megabytes of photos, work best on a computer rather than a phone. - q: Will the PDF be smaller than my photos? a: No. JPEG photos are embedded unchanged to avoid any quality loss, so the PDF is about the size of the photos combined. Resize or compress the images first if you need a smaller file. - q: Why did some pages come out in landscape? a: With orientation set to Auto, each page follows its image, so wide images get landscape pages. Choose Portrait or Landscape to make every page face the same way. - q: Can I convert HEIC photos from an iPhone? a: On an iPhone or iPad it usually just works, because iOS hands photos to websites as JPEG. Chrome and Firefox on Windows and Android cannot open HEIC files, so convert them to JPEG first or set the iPhone camera format to Most Compatible. ---
