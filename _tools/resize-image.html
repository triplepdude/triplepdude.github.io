Runs in your browser, no upload. category: Image & PDF keywords: [resize image, image resizer, resize photo, change image size, resize image to 1080x1080, resize image in pixels, resize image by percentage, reduce image dimensions] howto: | ## How to resize an image 1. Choose an image, drop it on the box or paste it with Ctrl+V (⌘V on a Mac). Any format your browser can display works, including JPG, PNG, WebP, GIF, BMP and AVIF. 2. Pick a preset, or type a width or height in pixels. With **Lock aspect ratio** on, the other side follows automatically so the picture is never squashed. Switch to **Percentage** to scale both sides at once, for example to 50%. 3. Choose the output format and quality, check the preview and the new file size, then press **Download**. ## When the new shape is different A preset such as 1080 × 1080 often has a different shape from your photo. Fixed-size presets unlock the aspect ratio, and you choose what happens to the difference: - **Crop to fill** scales the image until it covers the whole frame and trims the overflow equally from both sides, so the centre stays in view. This is what you want for social media posts and profile pictures. - **Fit inside** shows the whole image and fills the empty bands with white, black or transparency (letterboxing). - **Stretch** forces the exact width and height and distorts the picture. It is rarely right, but some upload forms insist on exact dimensions. ## How the resizing works Your browser decodes the image and draws it onto an HTML canvas at the new size with its best smoothing filter (`imageSmoothingQuality` set to `high`). For big reductions each side is first halved step by step until it is at most twice its target size, and only then scaled to the final size. Each halving averages neighbouring pixels (roughly a 2 × 2 block per output pixel), so fine detail such as hair, text and fabric does not break up into jagged edges and moiré. That breakup is what a single large step with a simple filter produces, because it samples only a few of the source pixels. Enlarging works too, but it cannot invent detail: the new pixels are interpolated from their neighbours, so edges look softer. For prints, a common rule is 300 pixels per inch, so a 6 × 4 inch print needs about 1800 × 1200 pixels. ## Formats, quality and file size | Format | Best for | Transparency | |---|---|---| | JPEG | Photos, smallest files | No, becomes white | | PNG | Screenshots, logos, text | Yes, lossless | | WebP | Photos and graphics for the web | Yes | {: .data .ri-table} The quality slider applies to JPEG and WebP. Settings from 80 to 90 usually look almost identical to the original at a fraction of the size, while below about 60 blocky artefacts start to show. PNG is lossless, so it ignores the slider. If your browser cannot encode WebP, the tool says so and saves a PNG instead. Saving a new file also drops the metadata stored in the original, such as the camera model and GPS location, which is worth knowing before you share a photo. Photos taken sideways are turned upright first, following their EXIF orientation tag, so the result is the way up you see it here. faq: - q: Is my image uploaded to a server? a: No. The image is decoded, resized and encoded by your own browser. Two exceptions are saving a photo as PNG, which is lossless and often several times larger than JPEG, and enlarging an image. The file size box shows the change before you download. - q: How do I resize to exactly 1080 × 1080 without squashing the photo? a: Choose the Instagram square preset, or unlock the aspect ratio and type 1080 in both boxes, then keep Crop to fill selected. The image is scaled to cover the square and the edges are trimmed evenly. - q: Can I use it for a passport or visa photo? a: The 600 × 600 preset matches the smallest square digital photo the US State Department accepts (it allows 600 to 1200 pixels per side). Upload portals also limit the file size, which is shown under the preview. The tool does not check head size, lighting or background, so compare the result with the official rules for your document. - q: Why does my enlarged image look blurry? a: Resizing cannot recreate detail that was never captured. When an image is enlarged, the extra pixels are estimated from their neighbours, which softens edges. Start from the largest original you have. ---
