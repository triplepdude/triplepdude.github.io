Many websites, older Windows PCs and editing apps still cannot open it, which is why converting to JPG is so common. ## How the conversion works The photo is decoded by libheif, an open-source HEIF library, compiled to WebAssembly and run in a background worker so the page stays responsive. The decoder, about 1.5 MB, is downloaded only when you add your first HEIC file, and your browser caches it. Your photos are never sent anywhere. - **Tiles.** A 12-megapixel iPhone photo is stored as a grid of 512 × 512 pixel tiles, which are joined back into one picture. - **Rotation.** The rotation and mirroring saved with the photo are applied, so portrait shots come out upright. - **Colour.** iPhone photos use the Display P3 colour space, which is wider than sRGB. The converter maps them to sRGB, the space that web browsers and most apps assume, so the JPG looks the same everywhere. Only the most saturated colours, beyond sRGB, lose a little intensity. - **Quality.** 90% keeps a JPG visually identical to the original for almost any photo. The JPG is usually larger than the HEIC, because HEVC compresses better than JPG does. ## Photo metadata (EXIF) | Setting | What the new file keeps | |---|---| | Remove all (default) | Nothing: no camera, date or location | | Keep, without location | Camera, lens, exposure and date; GPS is removed | | Keep all | Everything, including where the photo was taken | When metadata is kept, the orientation tag is reset to *normal*, because the pixels are already upright; otherwise some apps would rotate the photo a second time.
