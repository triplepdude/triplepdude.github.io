In the UK and India the line usually ends with "only". Either way, draw a line through any space you leave, so that nothing can be added. The words matter most: in the US (UCC 3-114), the UK (Bills of Exchange Act 1882, section 9) and India (Negotiable Instruments Act 1881, section 18), the amount in words is the one that counts when it differs from the figures. ## American and British English British English puts "and" after the hundreds and before the last part of a number: one hundred and five, two thousand and twenty-six. American English usually leaves it out and keeps "and" for the cents. Both write the numbers from 21 to 99 with a hyphen. A billion is a thousand million in both. Older British usage made it a million million (the long scale) until December 1974, when Prime Minister Harold Wilson told Parliament that ministers would use the international meaning. Choose **Thousand million** to see the long scale, or **Lakh, crore** for the Indian system, where 1,00,000 is one lakh and 1,00,00,000 one crore. ## What comes after a trillion? Each name is a thousand times the one before it: | Name | Zeros | Name | Zeros | |---|---|---|---| | million | 6 | sextillion | 21 | | billion | 9 | septillion | 24 | | trillion | 12 | octillion | 27 | | quadrillion | 15 | nonillion | 30 | | quintillion | 18 | decillion | 33 | The names go on with undecillion (36 zeros), duodecillion, tredecillion, quattuordecillion, quindecillion, sexdecillion, septendecillion, octodecillion and novemdecillion, up to vigintillion, a 1 with 63 zeros. ## Decimals and ordinals Decimals are read digit by digit after "point" (3.14 is three point one four) or as a fraction (three and fourteen hundredths). An ordinal changes only the last word: one becomes first, two second, three third, five fifth, eight eighth, nine ninth and twelve twelfth, a word ending in -y takes -ieth (twentieth), and the rest add -th (one hundred and fourth). The ordinal in figures above follows this rule for any number. - q: Should I write numbers in words or in figures? a: That depends on the style guide. AP style spells out one to nine and uses figures from 10, while the Chicago Manual of Style spells out whole numbers from zero to one hundred. Both spell out a number that starts a sentence. - q: Is it zero, nought or oh? a: All three name the digit 0. Zero is usual in American English and in science, nought is common in British English (nought point five), and oh is used when digits are read aloud, as in phone numbers and the year 1906 (nineteen oh six). - q: Is my number sent anywhere? a: No. The words are worked out by JavaScript in your browser, digit by digit, so even 66-digit numbers stay exact.
