A smaller numeral before a larger one is subtracted, which gives six pairs: IV (4), IX (9), XL (40), XC (90), CD (400) and CM (900). So: - a numeral is used at most three times in a row; - V, L and D are never repeated; - only I, X and C subtract, and only from the next two numerals up, at most ten times their value: I from V and X, X from L and C, C from D and M; - only one numeral is subtracted at a time. A numeral that breaks a rule still gets its likely value, the standard form and the rule it breaks. Many such forms are historic rather than wrong: Roman inscriptions have IIX for 8, and many clock faces show IIII. ## How do you write 2027 in Roman numerals? 2027 = 2000 + 20 + 7 = MM + XX + VII = **MMXXVII**. 2025 is MMXXV, 2026 is MMXXVI and 2028 is MMXXVIII; the chart lists every year from 1950 to 2035. ## Why is 99 XCIX, not IC? I is only put before V and X, so 99 is written as 90 + 9, XC + IX. IC breaks the rule that a numeral subtracts from no more than ten times its value. ## How do you write 5,000 or a million? A bar over a numeral, the vinculum, multiplies it by 1,000: V̅ is 5,000, X̅ 10,000 and M̅ 1,000,000. From 4,000 on, this converter writes the thousands with bars, so 4,000 is I̅V̅, as Wikipedia writes it; MV̅, which some sites use, is read as well. The largest number written with single bars is 3,999,999: M̅M̅M̅C̅M̅X̅C̅I̅X̅CMXCIX. **Copy** puts a combining overline after each barred letter, which some fonts draw unevenly; the button beside it gives the underscore form that some calculators use (_I_V for 4,000). ## Dates and birthdays Each part of a date becomes its own numeral: 14 April 2027 is XIV·IV·MMXXVII in day-month-year order, used in the UK, and IV·XIV·MMXXVII in the US order. Dots, slashes or spaces work as separators too. Before a tattoo, check the order: IV·V·MMXXVII is 4 May in the UK but 5 April in the US. The **Roman date** box reads a date back and checks that it exists. The usual explanations are tradition and the visual balance of IIII opposite VIII. The story that Louis XIV ordered IIII is told of other monarchs too. - q: Is there a Roman numeral for zero? a: No. The Romans had no numeral for zero.
