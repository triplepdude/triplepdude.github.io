Nothing is sent or stored. category: Security & Network keywords: [password strength checker, how strong is my password, password checker, password strength test, how long to crack my password, zxcvbn password strength, password entropy calculator] howto: | ## How to use it Type or paste a password into the box. It is checked on your device as you type, and the result shows a score from 0 to 4, the number of guesses an attacker would need, how long that takes in four attack scenarios, and which parts of the password make it weak. The matched parts stay hidden until you press **Show**. Under **Personal words** you can add your name, username or birthday: a password containing one of them counts as easy to guess, because an attacker who targets you would try them first. ## How the estimate works The checker uses zxcvbn, an estimator first published by Dropbox, in its zxcvbn-ts version. Rather than counting character types, it looks for the patterns that password-cracking tools try first: about 49,000 common passwords, the 7,776 Diceware words, English words, first names and surnames, Wikipedia terms, keyboard runs such as `qwerty` or `!@#$`, repeats (`aaa`, `abcabc`), sequences (`abcd`, `9876`), dates and recent years, reversed words and l33t swaps such as `@` for `a`. It then finds the cheapest way to build the whole password from those pieces plus random characters and multiplies the guesses each piece needs. | Score | Estimated guesses | What it resists | |---|---|---| | 0 | under 1,000 | Nothing; it is among the first guesses | | 1 | under 1 million | Online attacks on a site that limits attempts | | 2 | under 100 million | Online attacks with no limit | | 3 | under 10 billion | A stolen database hashed with a slow hash | | 4 | 10 billion or more | Strong protection even offline | Only the first 256 characters are analysed. ## Entropy versus pattern-based estimates The classic "entropy" formula multiplies the length by log₂ of the size of the character set used: 26 lowercase, 26 uppercase, 10 digits and 32 ASCII symbols, plus 1 for a space (this page counts any other character, such as é or 字, as 100 more). By that formula `P@ssw0rd123!` has 78.7 bits, because it uses all four kinds of character. zxcvbn sees through the capital and the two predictable swaps to *password123*, number 796 on its list of common passwords, allows for the final `!`, and estimates about 150,000 guesses, or 17 bits. The formula only fits passwords that a computer picked at random. For passwords people choose, it overstates the strength badly, which is why NIST SP 800-63B asks services to check new passwords against lists of common and breached ones instead of demanding a mix of character types. The page shows both numbers side by side. ## The four attack scenarios | Scenario | Guesses per second | Example | |---|---|---| | Online, throttled | 100 per hour | A login page that locks or slows down after failures | | Online, not throttled | 10 | A login page with no limit | | Offline, slow hash | 10,000 | A stolen database hashed with bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2 or PBKDF2 | | Offline, fast hash | 10 billion | A stolen database hashed with a fast hash such as MD5 or SHA-1 | These are the rates zxcvbn assumes, so treat the times as rough orders of magnitude. Real attackers can be faster: in hashcat benchmarks one RTX 4090 graphics card tests about 150 billion MD5 hashes per second. "Centuries" means 100 years or more. ## Tips - Let a password manager generate and remember a different random password for every site. - For the few passwords you must remember, use five or six random words. The [passphrase generator](/passphrase-generator/) picks them for you with your browser's secure random number generator. - A high score does not help once a password has leaked, so never reuse one, and turn on two-factor authentication where you can. faq: - q: Is my password sent anywhere? a: No. The word lists are downloaded once and the check runs in your browser. The password is never sent, logged or stored, and once the page has loaded the lists you can even go offline and keep checking. - q: Why is my long password with symbols rated weak? a: Length and symbols only help when the password is unpredictable. A common word with swaps such as @ for a and 0 for o, followed by digits or a year, is one of the first things cracking tools try, so it needs few guesses however complex it looks. - q: Does this check whether my password was in a data breach? a: Only partly. It recognises about 49,000 of the most common passwords, but it does not search databases of breached passwords, which would mean sending data over the network. A password that scores well can still have leaked if you used it elsewhere. - q: How long would it take to crack my password? a: See the crack-time table. It depends on the attack. The same password can take hours to guess through a login page that limits attempts, and well under a second offline when a site stored it with a fast hash such as MD5. ---
