A turn that is not a multiple of 90° leaves empty corners: - **Enlarge the canvas** keeps the whole picture and fills the corners with a colour or leaves them transparent. The new width is w × |cos θ| + h × |sin θ|, so a 4000 × 3000 photo turned 10° becomes 4460 × 3649 pixels. - **Crop off the corners** keeps the shape of the original and cuts the largest rectangle with no empty corners, the way you straighten a horizon. The same photo turned 2° keeps 3821 × 2866 pixels, 91% of it. ## Why photos turn sideways (EXIF orientation) Phones and cameras usually store a photo the way the sensor read it and add an EXIF Orientation tag, a number from 1 to 8 that says how to show it: 6 means turn 90° clockwise, 3 means 180°, and 2, 4, 5 and 7 add a mirror. Browsers have followed the tag since 2020, but some older programs, printers and upload forms ignore it and show the photo on its side. This tool applies the tag first, so you work on the picture you see, and the saved file holds upright pixels with no tag, so it looks the same everywhere. ## Does flipping reduce quality? A flip or a 90° turn moves every pixel to a new place without changing it. Saving is what can cost quality: PNG stores pixels without loss, while JPEG and WebP compress the picture again. With **Keep each JPEG's quality** on, the tool reads the original's quantisation tables, estimates the quality it was saved at (75 or 90, say) and saves at the same setting, so the file is not bloated by a higher setting or degraded by a lower one. Turning by other angles blends neighbouring pixels, which softens fine detail slightly. For a strictly lossless JPEG rotation, desktop tools such as jpegtran rearrange the compressed data itself; that is exact only when the width and height are multiples of the 8 or 16 pixel blocks JPEG uses. The new files carry no metadata such as the camera model or GPS location.
