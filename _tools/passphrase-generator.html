See entropy and crack time instantly. category: Generators keywords: [passphrase generator, diceware password generator, eff wordlist, random word password generator, memorable password generator, xkcd password generator, strong passphrase] howto: | ## How to use it Five passphrases appear as soon as the page loads. Pick one you like and copy it, or press **Generate new** for another batch. Changing any setting makes a fresh batch straight away. - **Words**: six is the usual recommendation. Each extra word multiplies the number of possible passphrases by 7,776. - **Separator** and **capitalisation** only change how the words are joined and written, to fit a site's password rules. They add no strength, because an attacker is assumed to know your settings. No word in the list is the start of another word, so even with no separator a passphrase splits back into its words only one way and loses nothing. - **Add a digit / a symbol** puts one random digit or one random symbol from `! # $ % & * + = ? @` at the end of a random word, for sites that insist on them. Each adds about 3.3 bits. ## Where the randomness comes from Words are drawn from the EFF Long Wordlist of 7,776 words, the list the Electronic Frontier Foundation published for Diceware, where five dice rolls (6⁵ = 7,776) pick each word. Here the dice are replaced by `crypto.getRandomValues`, the browser's cryptographically secure random number generator. A random 32-bit number is only used if it falls below the largest multiple of 7,776 that fits in 32 bits; otherwise it is thrown away and a new one is drawn. This *rejection sampling* avoids the small bias that a plain remainder would give, so every word is exactly equally likely. Nothing is predictable from the time, and nothing is sent anywhere. ## How strength is calculated Strength is measured in bits of entropy, assuming the attacker knows the word list and every setting you chose. Each word adds log2(7,776) ≈ 12.925 bits. Writing one random word in capitals adds log2 of the number of words, and a digit or a symbol adds log2(10) ≈ 3.32 bits each. Where the digit or symbol lands is not counted, which keeps the estimate on the safe side. | Words | Entropy | Average time at 100 billion guesses a second | |---|---|---| | 4 | 51.7 bits | about 5 hours | | 5 | 64.6 bits | about 4.5 years | | 6 | 77.5 bits | about 35 thousand years | | 7 | 90.5 bits | about 270 million years | The crack time is the average: half of all possible passphrases, divided by the guessing speed you choose. 100 billion guesses per second is roughly one high-end graphics card attacking a fast hash such as MD5. A login page that limits attempts is millions of times slower, while a site that stores passwords with a slow hash such as bcrypt or Argon2 slows offline attacks enormously. ## Tips - Use a passphrase for the few secrets you must remember: your password manager, computer login and email. Let the password manager create the rest. - Do not swap words for ones you like better or reorder them to form a sentence. Any human choice reduces the randomness. - Prefer real dice? Roll five dice per word and type the results into the lookup box to get the matching words from the same list. ## Word list credit The EFF Long Wordlist is by the Electronic Frontier Foundation (eff.org), published under the Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 United States licence (CC BY 3.0 US). This page uses the words unchanged, without the dice numbers. faq: - q: Is it safe to generate a passphrase on a website? a: This page makes passphrases entirely in your browser with the Web Crypto random generator. They are never sent to a server, logged or stored, and they are gone when you close the tab. You can disconnect from the internet after the page loads and it keeps working. - q: How many words should my passphrase have? a: Six words (about 77.5 bits) is a good default for important accounts such as a password manager. Five is reasonable where attempts are rate-limited, and seven or more suits encryption keys that could face offline attacks. - q: Is a passphrase better than a random password? a: Bit for bit they are equally strong. A six-word passphrase and a 12-character random password of letters, digits and symbols both have about 77 to 79 bits of entropy, but the words are far easier to remember and type. - q: Why does adding a capital letter or a separator not increase the strength? a: Strength counts only the random choices. If every word is capitalised, or the words are always joined with a hyphen, an attacker who knows the method tries that form first, so it adds nothing. Writing one randomly chosen word in capitals does add a little, and the entropy figure includes it. - q: What is the EFF word list? a: A list of 7,776 common English words chosen by the Electronic Frontier Foundation for Diceware, with offensive and hard-to-spell words removed. EFF also publishes shorter lists for four dice; this page uses the long list, the one intended for five dice. ---
