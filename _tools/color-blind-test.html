Get the screen right first. Night modes, blue-light and colour filters tint every plate, and a dim screen or glare washes the colours out, so go through the checklist above the Start button. 2. Press **Start the test**. Each plate hides a number, or a shape if you choose **Shapes** for a child who does not know numbers yet. 3. Tap or click what you see, or press its key. If you see nothing, choose **I see no number**: a guess only blurs the result. You get 7 seconds per plate, which leaves time to tap; the printed Ishihara test asks for an answer within 3 seconds. Choose 15 seconds or no limit if you need longer, but staring at a plate can make the faint ones easier. ## How the plates work Shinobu Ishihara's plates, first published in 1917, hide a number among dots of many sizes and shades, so the figure stands out by its colour only. The full edition has 38 plates, read at 75 cm in daylight. This test works the same way, but every plate is drawn afresh in your browser, so there is nothing to memorise. On each test plate the figure differs from its background in one type of cone only: the long- (L, red), medium- (M, green) or short-wavelength (S, blue) cones, worked out with Smith and Pokorny's cone sensitivities for a standard sRGB screen. Such colours lie on a *confusion line*: to someone without that cone type, figure and background are the same colour. There are four plates for each cone type, from bold to very faint, and two control plates with the figure in darker dots, which everyone should see. Ishihara's test checks red-green vision only; the S-cone plates here also screen for blue-yellow (tritan) deficiency. ## Reading your result | Plates of one kind missed | Severity hint | |---|---| | 0 | No sign of a deficiency | | 1 | No clear sign: often a mistap, the screen or the lighting | | 2 | Mild | | 3 | Moderate | | 4 | Strong, as in dichromacy | The likely type is the kind of plate you missed more often: protan (L cones) or deutan (M cones). If you miss as many of one kind as of the other, the result says the type is unclear. Two or more missed S-cone plates point to a tritan (blue-yellow) deficiency, which a warm-tinted screen can also cause. A missed control plate makes the whole result unreliable. ## Types of colour vision deficiency | Type | Cones | How common | |---|---|---| | Deuteranomaly | M cones shifted | About 5% of men | | Deuteranopia | No working M cones | About 1% of men | | Protanomaly | L cones shifted | About 1% of men | | Protanopia | No working L cones | About 1% of men | | Tritan | S cones | Fewer than 1 in 10,000 people | Red-green deficiency is inherited on the X chromosome, so among people of Northern European ancestry it affects about 1 in 12 men but only 1 in 200 women. Protans also see reds as darker. Blue-yellow deficiency affects men and women equally, and blue-yellow losses are often acquired rather than inherited, for example as the lens of the eye yellows with age. ## A screening, not a diagnosis A screen test can point to a deficiency but cannot diagnose one. An optometrist tests with printed plates under standard lighting, and specialist clinics use an anomaloscope, the most accurate instrument, to find the type and severity of a red-green deficiency. See one if your result shows signs of a deficiency, and promptly if your colour vision changes, above all in one eye only. To read the exact colour of anything in a photo, use the [image colour picker](/image-color-picker/). A plain [white screen](/white-screen/) makes a tint from Night Shift or a similar filter easy to spot before you start. faq: - q: Is my result uploaded or saved? a: No. The plates are drawn and scored in your browser with JavaScript. Your answers are not sent anywhere or stored, and they are gone when you close or reload the page, so use Copy result if you want to keep them. - q: Why can my result change on another screen or with Night Shift? a: The plates are worked out for a standard sRGB screen. Real screens differ in their colours, white and brightness, and Night Shift, Night Light, True Tone and colour filters change the balance of red, green and blue, so a figure meant to be hidden from one cone type can show faintly on one screen and vanish on another. Looking at a laptop screen from an angle shifts its colours too. - q: What is the difference between protanopia and deuteranopia? a: Both are red-green dichromacy, in which only two of the three cone types work. In protanopia the long-wavelength (red) cones are missing, so reds also look dark; in deuteranopia the medium-wavelength (green) cones are missing and reds keep their normal brightness. Protanomaly and deuteranomaly are the milder forms, where that cone type is present but its sensitivity is shifted towards the other. - q: Can a child take this test? a: Yes. Choose Shapes, and the child names or points to a circle, square, triangle or star, as in the shape plates eye care professionals use for young children. Choose No time limit if the child needs longer, and read a result for a child as a reason to arrange a proper test. - q: Can colour blindness be cured? a: "Inherited colour blindness has no cure, but it does not get worse over the years. Tinted glasses such as EnChroma make some colours stand out more for some people with a red-green deficiency, but they do not restore normal colour vision, and in studies wearers did no better on the Ishihara plates with them. Colour vision lost through an eye disease such as a cataract, or through a medicine, can improve once the cause is treated, which is one more reason to see an optometrist about any change." ---

Before you start

  • Turn off Night Shift, Night Light, blue-light filters, eye-comfort or reading modes, colour filters and inverted colours.
  • Set the brightness to at least half, in ordinary room light with no glare on the screen.
  • Sit at arm's length, straight in front of the screen, with your usual glasses but no tinted lenses.
