JPGs taken from web pages are compressed already, and a WebP at a high quality can be larger than they are, which is why each file shows its own result. ## Generation loss A JPG's pixels have already lost detail to compression. Converting to WebP compresses them a second time, so a little more is lost: at 80% the WebP differed from the JPG by about 36 to 42 dB PSNR in tests, which is hard to see on a screen but is a real change. That suits pictures for a website. For archives, keep the original JPGs, and convert from them rather than from an earlier WebP. There is no lossless mode here: a lossless WebP stores the JPG's compression noise exactly and came out 3 to 10 times the size of the JPG. ## Which quality to choose Between 75 and 85 suits most web pages; 80 is the default. Going higher rarely helps a JPG, because the detail the extra bytes would keep is gone already. If a file comes out larger, lower the quality or keep the JPG. ## Metadata, orientation and colours - **EXIF** (camera, date taken) is kept without the GPS location by default; the **Photo metadata** menu can remove all or keep all. Some apps ignore EXIF inside a WebP file. - **Orientation**: a photo stored sideways is turned upright in the pixels, so it shows correctly everywhere. - **Colour profiles** of JPGs, such as Display P3 from an iPhone or Adobe RGB from a camera, are copied into the WebP, so the colours stay as saturated as in the JPG. HEIC photos, and CMYK JPGs made for print, become sRGB. For PNG files, [PNG to WebP](/png-to-webp/) offers a lossless mode.
